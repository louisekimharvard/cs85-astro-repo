The Stellar Graveyard

When very big stars die, they leave behind black holes and neutron stars. Sometimes two of these dead stars orbit each other, spiral closer, and crash together. The crash shakes space itself, and detectors on Earth can feel it. This page uses every confident detection so far to ask: how heavy are these objects, and are there weights nature seems to skip?

Illustration, not data

A chirp from across the universe

As the two objects spiral inward they orbit faster and faster. The ripples they send out in space (gravitational waves) speed up and get stronger, then stop when the objects merge. Turned into sound, the signal is a short rising "whoop", which astronomers call a chirp.

The basics

What exactly collided?

Astronomers weigh stars in solar masses: one solar mass is the mass of our Sun. Every collision in this catalog involves two of these leftovers:

A rough rule: anything lighter than about 3 solar masses is a neutron star, anything heavier is a black hole. We can't see these objects with telescopes. We work out their masses from the shape of the gravitational-wave signal: heavier pairs produce lower-pitched, shorter chirps.

Are there weights nature skips?

Every collision has two objects, so 282 collisions with published masses give us 564 dead stars. Below, each bar counts how many fall in a mass range. The scale is stretched so that small and huge masses both fit: each step to the right is a bigger jump in mass than the last.

Physicists predicted two ranges where objects should be rare. They're shaded in purple. Tap any bar to see which collisions are in it.

LOWER MASS GAP · 3–5 SOLAR MASSES 1 object

The heaviest known neutron stars are about 2–2.5 solar masses, and the lightest black holes in our galaxy are about 5. Almost nothing turns up in between. It's still an open question whether stars just don't make objects this size.

PAIR-INSTABILITY GAP · 50–130 SOLAR MASSES 60 objects

Theory says giant stars that would leave black holes this heavy blow themselves apart completely, leaving nothing. Yet LIGO keeps finding black holes here. One idea is that they were built from earlier collisions, like a black hole eating another.

Chart 4 · Near and far

Why the heavy ones seem farther away

When two objects merge, they become one. The new object is a little lighter than the two added together: about 2.5 solar masses (around 4%) typically vanishes, turned into the energy of the gravitational waves.

Here each dot is that final object, placed by how far away the collision happened. Heavy collisions are "loud" and can be heard from billions of light-years away. Light ones are quiet, so we only hear them when they're close. That's why the bottom right is empty. It shows the limits of our detectors, not a real lack of collisions.

Glossary

Words on this page

Gravitational wave

A ripple in space itself, predicted by Einstein in 1916. Passing waves stretch and squeeze everything by a tiny amount: LIGO measures changes in its 4 km arms far smaller than a proton.

LIGO, Virgo, KAGRA

Gravitational-wave detectors in the USA (two LIGO sites), Italy (Virgo) and Japan (KAGRA). They work together, and the catalog is their shared list of detections.

Solar mass

The mass of our Sun, about 2 × 1030 kg, or 333,000 Earths.

Effective spin

One number summarising the two objects' spins: from −1 (spinning fully against the orbit) through 0 to +1 (spinning fully along it). Pairs that formed together tend to spin along their orbit; pairs that met by chance in a crowded star cluster can point any way.

Uncertainty

Masses come from fitting the signal, which is noisy, so each comes with a range. "34.6 (+4.4 / −2.6)" means the true mass is very likely between 32.0 and 39.0 solar masses.

Summing up

What the graveyard tells us

Black holes are common

273 of 282 weighed collisions were two black holes, most of them 20–40 times our Sun's mass.

Neutron-star crashes are rare finds

Only 9 involved a neutron star, partly because they're quiet and only heard nearby.

One gap looks real

Almost nothing sits between 3 and 5 solar masses.

One gap isn't empty

Dozens of black holes sit in the 50–130 range theory said should be bare, though many are within their error ranges of its edge.
